**How Digital Mediation Shapes Loyalty in Festival Experiencescapes
Through Personalization and Immersion**

Festivals are increasingly experienced through hybrid infrastructures in
which physical co-presence is intertwined with digital touchpoints. This
paper examines how smart festival technologies become meaningful within
digitally mediated festival experiencescapes and how they shape
post-experiential outcomes. Drawing on survey data from 216 festival
attendees with direct prior use of smart or emerging technologies, the
study uses covariance-based structural equation modelling to test a
model linking perceived usefulness, trust in smart technologies, and
privacy concerns to perceived personalization, immersive experience, and
behavioral loyalty intention. The findings show that perceived
usefulness and trust positively influence perceived personalization,
which in turn strengthens immersive experience and, ultimately,
loyalty-related intentions. Privacy concerns do not significantly reduce
perceived personalization in this sample. The paper argues that the
value of smart festival technologies lies less in technological novelty
itself than in their capacity to make the festival environment feel more
relevant, navigable, and emotionally engaging. In doing so, it
contributes by positioning smart festival technologies as a mediating
layer within the festival experiencescape and by showing how
personalization and immersion operate as key mechanisms linking
technology evaluations to loyalty.

Keywords: smart festival technologies; festival experiencescape;
personalization; immersive experience; loyalty; digital mediation

# Introduction

Festivals are increasingly experienced through hybrid infrastructures
that combine physical co-presence with digitally mediated event systems
(Ryan et al., 2020). In these settings, technology does not merely
support transactions or logistics; it shapes how attendees move through
the venue, make decisions, discover content, and engage with the event
environment in real time (Hudson & Hudson, 2013; Dieck et al., 2021).
This is especially important in festival contexts, where participation
is temporally compressed, spatially distributed, and often characterised
by simultaneity, crowding, and rapid micro-decisions. From this
perspective, festivals can be understood as digitally mediated
experiencescapes in which technological systems increasingly participate
in the production of attention, movement, interaction, and affective
engagement (Chen et al., 2025; Cimbaljević et al., 2021; Mieli et al.,
2024; Sinanan & Ritter, 2024).

Despite growing research on tourism and event technologies, two
limitations remain. On the one hand, technology-oriented studies have
often treated adoption-related beliefs, such as usefulness, trust, and
perceived risk, as outcomes in themselves. On the other hand,
experiencescape-oriented work has more strongly emphasised experiential
quality, immersion, and loyalty, but has not always modelled the
technological conditions through which those outcomes emerge. This
leaves an important gap in understanding how evaluations of smart
festival technologies become translated into situated experiential
mechanisms within the festival experiencescape, particularly through
perceived personalization and immersive experience (Jeong & Shin, 2020;
Sisson & Alcorn, 2022).

This gap matters because the value of smart festival technologies is not
automatic. It depends on whether attendees perceive them as useful and
trustworthy, and on whether data-related concerns undermine their
legitimacy (Hur et al., 2022). At the same time, personalization may be
the key mechanism through which the smart layer becomes experientially
meaningful, making the festival environment feel more relevant,
navigable, and individually responsive (Singh et al., 2021).
Personalization may then intensify immersion, understood here as a
deeper presence-in-place within the digitally mediated festival setting,
while loyalty can be interpreted as a post-experiential outcome
expressed through revisit and recommendation intentions (Celuch., 2021).
Recent festival research has begun to show that AI-enabled technologies
can influence post-event outcomes such as eWOM, memorable experiences,
and well-being, yet the sequential pathway through which technology
evaluations shape personalization, immersion, and loyalty in festival
experiencescapes remains underexplored (Lopes et al., 2026).

To address this gap, this study develops and tests a covariance-based
structural equation model linking perceived usefulness, trust in smart
festival technologies, and privacy concerns to perceived
personalization, immersive experience, and behavioral loyalty intention.
The study contributes by shifting attention from technology adoption as
an end in itself toward a more experiential and spatial account of how
smart festival infrastructures become meaningful through personalization
and immersion.

# Literature review and hypotheses development

## Festivals as experiencescapes and temporary geographies

Festivals are commonly theorised as temporary experiential environments
where spatial design, social density and atmospheric cues co-produce
intense, time-bounded encounters. In event-tourism terms, these settings
operate as experiencescapes in which meaning is not merely delivered but
co-created through participant interaction and situated practices (Chen
et al., 2025; Sisson & Alcorn, 2022). Building on the service
environment tradition initiated by Bitner (1992), later work has argued
for broader and more experiential readings of service environments that
extend beyond purely physical surroundings to encompass more holistic
constellations of stimuli and stakeholder experience (O'Dell, 2005;
Pizam & Tasci, 2019). From this perspective, a festivalscape lens
highlights how layout, symbolic design, social density and mediated
touchpoints shape affective responses and downstream loyalty-related
intentions (Sisson & Alcorn, 2022; Molina-Gómez et al., 2021). Crucially
for a geographic reading, festivals are liminal and highly relational
spaces in which participants temporarily rework identities, attachments
and modes of presence in place (Jaimangal-Jones et al., 2010; Wu et al.,
2020). These socio-spatial dynamics provide the baseline against which
digital mediation can either support co-creation or introduce new
frictions (Azara et al., 2023).

## Digital mediation and technology-facilitated engagement

Recent research shows that digital infrastructures increasingly mediate
event engagement across pre-event anticipation, in-event navigation, and
post-event memory practices (Chen et al., 2025). In festivals, mobile
and location-aware systems extend the experience beyond physical
boundaries, enabling shared documentation and post-event connections,
while smart infrastructures, such as apps, cashless payments, and
real-time updates, influence on-site movement and coordination (Wood &
Kinnunen, 2020; Cimbaljević et al., 2021; Duignan et al., 2018). 

Festivals settings are marked by time-compressed, spatially distributed
engagement amid crowding, simultaneity, and rapid micro-decisions. Here,
technology supports coordination, reduces friction, and extends the
experience across pre-event planning, on-site participation, and
post-event interaction (Van Winkle et al., 2018). Mieli et al. (2024)
conceptualise this process as a phygital reconfiguration of tourist
space-time, while Sinanan et al. (2024) show how emerging media
technologies increasingly shape the ways visitors move through,
interpret, and connect with places.

## Technology evaluations as conditions of a smart experiencescape

In digitally mediated experiencescapes, the value of technologies
depends less on adoption itself than on participants\' practical
evaluations of their usefulness, trustworthiness, and data implications.
Studies on technology-enhanced events and tourism show that engagement
with smart systems is shaped not only by perceived benefits and enabling
conditions, but also by concerns about data practices and technological
risks (Sjukriana et al., 2024; García Revilla et al., 2023).

Perceived usefulness is the degree to which attendees believe smart
festival technologies improve their experience, making it more
efficient, relevant, convenient, or responsive to needs. For instance,
in festival contexts, technologies such as official applications,
digital ticketing, push notifications, and navigation tools alleviate
logistical frictions, thereby facilitating advanced planning, spatial
orientation, and instantaneous decision-making. In addition to these
utilitarian advantages, they enhance situational relevance and
attendees\' perceived agency across the entire event space (Talantis et
al., 2020; Ozturk et al., 2020).

Trust is a second key factor in attendees\' evaluation of smart
infrastructure, supporting perceptions of reliability and risk reduction
in dynamic, high-stakes festival settings. Attendees rely on
technologies from organizers, platforms, or third-party providers, so
trust is essential for viewing these systems as reliable and beneficial.
Research on event ecosystems shows that reliability and performance
boost satisfaction and confidence (Neuhofer et al., 2021), while trust
links usability beliefs to behavioral intentions (Hua et al., 2021).

Privacy concerns refer to attendees\' unease regarding the collection,
tracking, and use of personal or location data via smart festival
technologies. In smart tourism contexts, such apprehensions often reduce
willingness to share information and limit engagement with personalized
services (Femenia et al., 2022; Ioannou et al., 2020). Perceptions of
privacy and security shape how technology features affect experiential
outcomes, acting as primary barriers in data-centric tourism (Jeong &
Shin, 2020; Zhang & Hara, 2024) . Yet, privacy and personalization do
not invariably conflict; privacy evaluations can align with
personalization benefits in data-intensive technologies (Ciftci et al.,
2024).

## Personalisation as spatial--experiential fit

In smart festival experiencescapes, personalisation operates as more
than a marketing tactic; it functions as a spatial--experiential
mechanism through which digitally mediated environments become more
navigable, relevant, and responsive to individual needs (Stankov et al.,
2018). Rather than simply tailoring promotional content, personalisation
helps attendees filter information, reduce overload, and make situated
decisions in complex and time-pressured festival settings.

Research in smart tourism has increasingly positioned recommendation
systems and context-aware digital services as central to experience
quality, particularly where travellers must process large volumes of
information and act in real time (Hardy et al., 2017). Personalised
systems can enhance perceived relevance by matching content,
suggestions, and routes to user preferences and situational context
(Qassimi & Rakrak, 2025; Du et al., 2018). In dynamic environments, this
may stabilise the user's trajectory through the experiencescape by
improving orientation, reducing friction, and supporting more confident
micro-decisions across the event space.

At the same time, the effectiveness of personalisation depends not only
on algorithmic relevance but also on how users interpret the quality and
legitimacy of what is being recommended. Prior research suggests that
information quality, interface cues, and the perceived credibility of
the system shape attitudinal responses to personalised recommendations
(Yang et al., 2023). From a spatial perspective, digital wayfinding
offers a particularly useful illustration of how context-aware
personalisation can be translated into navigational support within
large-scale environments, helping users move through space in ways that
feel more accessible and manageable (Wheeler et al., 2020).

In festival contexts, where attendees face crowding, temporal
simultaneity, and multiple experiential options, perceived
personalisation may therefore become a key mechanism through which the
smart layer acquires experiential value. When technologies are
experienced as capable of aligning the event environment with individual
interests and situational needs, they are more likely to support deeper
engagement with the festival setting (Van Winkle et al., 2018).

## Immersion as presence-in-place in hybrid environments

Immersion can be conceptualised as a state of presence-in-place within a
hybrid physical--digital environment where attention, affect and sensory
engagement become focused on the situated encounter. Tourism research
links spatial presence to enjoyment, destination image and behavioural
intentions (Kim et al., 2021), and distinguishes spatial presence as a
key dimension alongside self- and social presence (Cho et al., 2002;
Hyun & O'Keefe, 2012). Telepresence further captures the psychological
sensation of 'being there' in mediated environments (Steuer, 1992;
Sheridan, 1992).

Interactive digital formats can foster immersion via visual vividness,
interactivity and flow processes. In tourism live streaming, visual
effects and interactivity can influence participation through spatial
presence and flow (Ye et al., 2022). Beyond AR/VR novelty, narrative
coherence and digital storytelling can deepen emotional connection and
engagement, increasing revisit-related intentions (Mo et al., 2024). In
hybrid event formats, immersion emerges from the integration of physical
and digital participation in the same experiential field (Georgoula et
al., 2025).

## Loyalty outcomes as spatially anchored behavioural intentions

Festival loyalty is commonly operationalised as revisit and
recommendation intentions, reflecting a durable relation to an
event-place assemblage over time. Classic consumer theory frames loyalty
as a sustained commitment despite situational pressures (Oliver, 1999).
In festival contexts, positive experience perceptions predict loyalty
through enhanced festival image and satisfaction (Dalgiç & Birdir, 2020;
Molina-Gómez et al., 2021). The experience economy perspective also
links festival experience dimensions to loyalty and word-of-mouth
outcomes (Sisson & Alcorn, 2022), suggesting that digitally mediated
personalisation and immersion can contribute to post-event advocacy and
return intentions.

This broader reading of loyalty is especially relevant when the event is
experienced as a spatially and affectively meaningful environment rather
than simply as a service product. Ong and Coetzee (2025) emphasise the
role of events in shaping identities, meanings, and place-related
connections, while Higham and Vada (2025) show how tourism experience,
place attachment, and destination loyalty are closely interrelated in
sport tourism geographies. In this sense, revisit and recommendation
intentions do not merely indicate satisfaction with an event product;
they also suggest that the festival has been consolidated in memory as a
desirable spatial--experiential assemblage to which attendees may wish
to return and which they may advocate to others.

## Integrated gap and hypotheses alignment

Contemporary festival research has begun to connect AI-enabled
technologies with post-experiential outcomes. Lopes et al. (2026) show
that festival-goers' perceptions of AI technologies, particularly
informativeness and interactivity, can stimulate eWOM, which in turn
fosters memorable experiences and tourists' well-being. While this work
advances understanding of how AI shapes communication and post-event
evaluation in festival settings, it leaves less clear how technology
evaluations become translated into situated experiential mechanisms
within the festival experiencescape itself. In particular, the roles of
perceived personalisation and immersion as sequential pathways linking
smart festival technologies to loyalty remain underexplored.

This study proposes an integrated framework where perceived
personalization acts as the key spatial--experiential mechanism,
rendering digitally mediated environments more significant, navigable,
and personally pertinent. Personalization is then expected to enhance
immersive experiences in the hybrid festival experiencescape, thereby
strengthening loyalty intentions toward the event.

On this basis, the following hypotheses are proposed:

(1) H1a. The perceived usefulness of smart festival technologies (PU) is
    positively associated with perceived personalisation (PERS).

(2) H1b. Trust in smart festival technologies and their providers (CONF)
    is positively associated with perceived personalisation (PERS).

(3) H1c. Privacy concerns regarding the collection and use of personal
    and location data (PRIV) are negatively associated with perceived
    personalisation (PERS).

(4) H2. Perceived personalisation (PERS) positively influences the
    immersive experience (INM) within the digitally mediated festival
    experiencescape.

(5) H3. The immersive experience (INM) positively influences loyalty to
    the event (LOY), operationalised as intention to revisit and
    recommend.

# Methods

## Sample and data collection

Data collection occurred between November 2025 and February 2026 via an
online questionnaire distributed through social media and live-festival
online communities. Non-probability purposive sampling was used.
Participation was voluntary, with informed consent prior to
participation and responses collected anonymously. To ensure festival
relevance, a screening question confirmed attendance at a live festival
in the past two years; non-qualifying participants were excluded.

The sample was restricted to respondents who reported direct use of
smart or emerging technologies in festival settings (n = 216). This
restriction was applied to ensure that evaluations of usefulness, trust,
privacy concerns, perceived personalization, and immersive experience
were grounded in actual technological exposure rather than hypothetical
assessment. The analytical sample was predominantly composed of
respondents reporting Spanish nationality, indicating that the study
mainly captures a Spain-centred festival audience. Respondents were
relatively young on average (mean age 26 years), and the sample was
slightly female-skewed (56.0% women; 42.1% men; remaining respondents
identified as non-binary, preferred not to say, or provided other
responses). Festival attendance was relatively frequent, with most
respondents reporting attendance either once a year (40.7%) or two to
three times a year (43.5%). In terms of festival genres, music festivals
clearly dominated the sample (89.8%), followed by arts (48.1%), food
(46.3%), and cultural festivals (39.4%). Overall, this profile suggests
a technology-exposed festival audience with strong representation of
high-density music-festival settings alongside other festival formats
(Table 1).

The technology profile---a multiple-response item (percentages do not
sum to 100%)---among the analytical sample suggests that respondents
were primarily exposed to functional and service-oriented smart festival
technologies rather than exclusively immersive or experimental formats.
The most frequently reported technologies were digital tickets (95.4%),
QR codes (94.0%), RFID wristbands or wearables (79.2%), and official
festival apps (76.4%). By contrast, more advanced or less widely
diffused technologies such as AI-based systems (28.7%), VR experiences
(19.9%), AR experiences (12.5%), robots (11.6%), and metaverse
experiences (5.6%) were substantially less common. This pattern
indicates that the empirical model captures digitally mediated festival
experiences in a broad sense, but within a usage landscape still
dominated by functional, navigational, and access-related technologies.
Accordingly, the findings should be interpreted as applying to
technology-exposed festival attendees and as reflecting aggregate
smart-technology-enabled festival experiences rather than the effects of
any single technological application.

## Questionnaire and measures

The questionnaire was designed to operationalise the six latent
constructs included in the proposed model using multi-item measures
adapted from established scales in prior research. All focal constructs
were measured on seven-point Likert-type scales ranging from 1
("strongly disagree") to 7 ("strongly agree"). Perceived usefulness was
measured with four items adapted from technology acceptance research in
digital event contexts, consistent with the original TAM logic (Davis,
1989; Bär et al., 2025). Perceived personalisation was measured with
four items capturing the extent to which festival technologies were
perceived to tailor information and recommendations and to support
user-controlled customisation, drawing on smart tourism and
personalisation research (Koo et al., 2025; Jeong & Shin, 2020).
Immersive experience was measured with three items assessing absorption,
emotional connection, and deep engagement enabled by immersive and
interactive festival touchpoints (Chen et al., 2025). Trust in smart
festival technologies was measured with three items adapted from the
integrated trust--TAM framework, reflecting beliefs about reliability,
dependability, and acting in the user's interest (Gefen et al., 2003).
Privacy and data concerns were measured with three items adapted from
the IUIPC scale, capturing apprehension regarding data collection,
sharing, and use (Malhotra et al., 2004). Finally, loyalty was measured
with three items assessing revisit and recommendation intentions toward
festivals that effectively integrate smart technologies into the
attendee experience (Lee et al., 2012; Jeong & Shin, 2020). The scales
were originally sourced from English-language studies, adapted into
Spanish for questionnaire administration, and contextually refined for
the festival setting. The English wording presented in this paper is
intended to reflect the meaning of the Spanish items used in the survey
instrument.

Prior to the main data collection, the questionnaire was piloted with 45
individuals who had experience attending live festivals. Following the
pilot, two academic reviewers assessed the instrument and minor
refinements were made to improve wording and construct clarity. All six
constructs retained for the present analysis were modelled as reflective
latent variables. In addition to the focal measures, the questionnaire
also included brief sections on festival attendance, prior exposure to
festival technologies, and respondents' socio-demographic profile.

A full list of measurement items, construct operationalisations, and
source scales is provided in Appendix Table A1.

## Data analysis

The main CFA and SEM models were estimated in R using lavaan with robust
maximum likelihood (MLR) estimation and FIML treatment of missing data.
This approach was considered appropriate because the study aimed to test
a theory-driven latent-variable model composed of reflective constructs
and to evaluate both measurement quality and structural relationships
simultaneously.

The measurement model was assessed by examining standardised factor
loadings, Cronbach's alpha, composite reliability (CR), and average
variance extracted (AVE). Convergent validity was considered
satisfactory when AVE exceeded 0.50 and CR exceeded 0.70 (Fornell &
Larcker, 1981), while discriminant validity was evaluated using the
heterotrait--monotrait ratio (HTMT), with values below 0.85 indicating
adequate discriminant validity (Henseler et al., 2015). Because all
constructs retained in the present study were specified as reflective
latent variables, this set of criteria was considered appropriate for
evaluating the psychometric quality of the model.

The structural model was then assessed by examining standardised path
coefficients, their significance levels, effect sizes (f²), and the
coefficients of determination (R²) of the endogenous constructs. Global
model fit was evaluated using multiple indices, including the chi-square
to degrees-of-freedom ratio (χ²/df), the Comparative Fit Index (CFI),
the Tucker--Lewis Index (TLI), the Root Mean Square Error of
Approximation (RMSEA), and the Standardised Root Mean Square Residual
(SRMR), following established SEM guidance (Hu & Bentler, 1999). As in
other applications of SEM in tourism research, fit indices were
interpreted jointly rather than in isolation.

Indirect effects were tested using bootstrap resampling with 5,000
resamples, allowing the estimation of standard errors, p-values, and
confidence intervals for the mediated paths. In addition, the
explanatory power of the model was assessed through the R² values of
perceived personalisation, immersive experience, and loyalty, while
substantive effect magnitudes were interpreted using f² values. Given
the moderate complexity of the model and the strength of the measurement
parameters, the analytical sample of 216 respondents was considered
adequate for the present CB-SEM application, while recognising that
sample adequacy in SEM is model-dependent rather than governed by a
universal rule (Wolf et al., 2013).

## Results 

Appendix Table A2 reports the descriptive statistics and standardized
loadings for all measurement items. Overall, the indicators displayed
satisfactory psychometric properties. All standardized factor loadings
were statistically significant at the 0.001 level, and most exceeded the
conventional 0.70 threshold. Only one item (Smart technologies would
improve my ability to navigate and plan my festival experience = 0.668)
fell slightly below this benchmark, although it remained statistically
significant and was retained because the corresponding construct still
demonstrated acceptable overall reliability and convergent validity. The
remaining loadings ranged from 0.732 to 0.930, indicating that the
observed indicators adequately represented their intended latent
constructs. 

The measurement model demonstrated satisfactory internal consistency and
convergent validity (Table 2). Average variance extracted (AVE) values
ranged from 0.607 to 0.799, consistently exceeding the recommended
threshold of 0.50. Composite reliability (CR) values ranged from 0.840
to 0.923, while Cronbach's alpha coefficients ranged from 0.837 to
0.922. Taken together, these results indicate that all constructs
exhibited acceptable-to-strong reliability and convergent validity. In
particular, privacy concerns, perceived personalization, immersive
experience, and loyalty showed especially strong psychometric
performance.

## Assessment of the measurement model 

The structural model results are presented in Table 4. Perceived
usefulness had a positive and statistically significant effect on
perceived personalization (β = 0.483, p \< 0.001), supporting H1a. Trust
also positively predicted perceived personalization (β = 0.329, p \<
0.001), thus supporting H1b. In contrast, privacy concerns did not
significantly influence perceived personalization (β = 0.096, p =
0.122), leading to the rejection of H1c. These findings suggest that,
among festival attendees with direct experience using smart
technologies, perceptions of usefulness and trust are meaningful
antecedents of personalization, whereas privacy concerns do not appear
to shape personalization perceptions in a statistically significant way.

The downstream experiential and behavioural relationships were strongly
supported. Perceived personalization exerted a strong positive influence
on immersive experience (β = 0.637, p \< 0.001), supporting H2.
Immersive experience, in turn, had a strong positive effect on loyalty
(β = 0.719, p \< 0.001), supporting H3. These results indicate that the
influence of smart festival technologies on loyalty is not merely
functional, but unfolds through an experiential pathway in which
personalization enhances immersion and immersion subsequently
strengthens loyalty-related intentions.

The model explained a substantial proportion of variance in the
endogenous constructs. Specifically, the predictors accounted for 56.3%
of the variance in perceived personalization, 40.5% of the variance in
immersive experience, and 51.7% of the variance in loyalty. These R²
values suggest that the model has moderate-to-strong explanatory power,
particularly for personalization and loyalty.

The effect size estimates further clarify the substantive contribution
of each path. The effect of perceived usefulness on personalization was
of medium magnitude (f² = 0.175), while the effect of trust on
personalization was small (f² = 0.089). Privacy concerns had only a
trivial effect size (f² = 0.013), consistent with the non-significant
path coefficient. By contrast, personalization had a large effect on
immersive experience (f² = 0.682), and immersive experience had a very
large effect on loyalty (f² = 1.070). These findings indicate that the
strongest part of the model lies in the experiential sequence linking
personalization, immersion, and loyalty. 

The measurement model demonstrated satisfactory psychometric properties,
whereas the structural model showed less-than-ideal global fit. In line
with this, the findings are interpreted primarily in terms of
measurement adequacy, explained variance, and the theoretical
consistency of the estimated structural relationships. Detailed fit
statistics are provided in Appendix Table A3.

Overall, four of the five hypothesized relationships were supported.
H1a, H1b, H2, and H3 were confirmed, whereas H1c was not supported.
Taken together, the results suggest that in digitally mediated festival
environments, perceived usefulness and trust enhance perceived
personalization, which in turn strengthens immersive experience and,
ultimately, loyalty toward the event. Privacy concerns, however, do not
appear to significantly undermine personalization in this sample of
technology-exposed festival attendees. 

## Indirect effects

To examine the mediated relationships in the model, bootstrap analyses
with 5,000 resamples were conducted. As shown in Table 5, perceived
usefulness had a positive and significant indirect effect on loyalty
through personalization and immersive experience (β = 0.221, p \< 0.001,
95% CI \[0.116, 0.353\]). Trust also exerted a positive and significant
indirect effect on loyalty through the same sequential pathway (β =
0.151, p = 0.001, 95% CI \[0.056, 0.262\]). In contrast, the indirect
effect of privacy concerns on loyalty was not statistically significant
(β = 0.044, p = 0.136, 95% CI \[-0.013, 0.102\]). These results
reinforce the view that usefulness and trust shape loyalty indirectly by
fostering personalization and immersive experience, whereas privacy
concerns do not appear to play a meaningful role in this mechanism.

# Discussion and implications

The findings suggest that smart festival technologies function less as
ends in themselves than as enabling conditions for more personalised and
immersive festival experiences. Perceived usefulness and trust both
positively predicted perceived personalization, which in turn strongly
enhanced immersive experience and behavioral loyalty intention. This
pattern suggests that the smart layer becomes valuable when it helps
attendees experience the festival as more personally relevant,
experientially coherent, and emotionally engaging, rather than simply
more technologically advanced. Smart festival technologies therefore
appear to matter less because of novelty itself than because of their
capacity to support meaningful experiential outcomes. This
interpretation is consistent with prior work linking smart tourism
technologies and festival experience to downstream behavioural
responses, as well as with recent festival research showing that
AI-related technology perceptions can shape memorable experiences and
well-being (Jeong & Shin, 2020; Sisson & Alcorn, 2022; Lopes et al.,
2026).

The strongest contribution of the model lies in the sequential pathway
from personalization to immersion and then to loyalty. Both the
explanatory power of the model and the effect-size estimates indicate
that the central mechanism is not a direct technology--loyalty
relationship, but an experiential sequence in which smart systems first
make the event feel more individually meaningful and then deepen
presence and involvement within the festival setting. The
indirect-effect analysis reinforces this interpretation: perceived
usefulness and trust influenced loyalty through personalization and
immersion, whereas privacy concerns did not. Accordingly, loyalty should
be understood less as a direct response to technological novelty and
more as the downstream consequence of a festival experience that feels
both tailored and immersive.

The non-significant role of privacy concerns is theoretically
noteworthy. Prior smart tourism research has often framed privacy as a
salient tension in data-driven environments (Femenia et al., 2022), yet
more recent work suggests that privacy and personalization do not always
operate as a simple zero-sum trade-off (Ciftci et al., 2024). The
present findings are consistent with this more contingent view. Among
technology-exposed festival attendees, privacy concerns may remain
present without necessarily undermining the perceived value of
personalization. One possible explanation is that, in temporally
compressed and operationally intense festival environments, users may
bracket privacy concerns when technologies are already familiar, useful,
and embedded in the practical flow of participation. This interpretation
also fits the technological profile of the sample, which was dominated
by normalized and service-oriented systems rather than more intrusive or
experimental technologies.

These conclusions should nevertheless be interpreted with caution.
Although the measurement model showed satisfactory psychometric quality,
the overall global fit of the structural model was less than ideal. For
this reason, the findings are best interpreted in terms of the
consistency of the estimated relationships, the explanatory power of the
model, and the substantive plausibility of the proposed sequence rather
than as evidence of a perfectly fitting structure. Even so, the results
offer a coherent account of how smart festival technologies can support
loyalty by intensifying personalised and immersive engagement within
hybrid, digitally mediated festival environments, which is consistent
with broader work on the phygital reconfiguration of tourist space and
experience (Mieli et al., 2024).

This study contributes to the literature by shifting attention from
technology adoption as an end in itself toward a more experiential and
spatial understanding of smart festival technologies within the festival
experiencescape. The findings indicate that the value of the smart layer
emerges through a sequential pathway in which perceived usefulness and
trust enhance perceived personalization, personalization strengthens
immersive experience, and immersion ultimately drives behavioral loyalty
intention. In doing so, the study bridges technology-oriented and
experiencescape-oriented research, repositions personalization as a
spatial--experiential mechanism rather than a purely marketing feature,
and suggests that privacy concerns may operate in a more contingent and
context-dependent way than is often assumed in data-driven tourism
settings.

For festival organizers, the results suggest that the strategic value of
smart technologies lies less in novelty alone than in their ability to
make the festival experience feel more relevant, navigable, and engaging
for attendees. Technologies that improve wayfinding, planning,
responsiveness, and experiential coherence are likely to generate
stronger downstream benefits than technologies introduced mainly as
symbolic innovation. The findings also highlight the importance of
trust: smart systems need to be reliable, easy to rely on under peak
demand, and visibly aligned with attendees' interests. In practical
terms, organizers may achieve greater loyalty-related outcomes by
improving the integration of everyday smart touchpoints rather than
focusing exclusively on more experimental technologies.

Despite these implications, some limitations should be acknowledged.
First, the study relied on purposive non-probability sampling and a
sample restricted to respondents with direct prior use of smart festival
technologies, so the findings should be interpreted as applying
primarily to technology-exposed festival attendees rather than to the
wider festival-going population. Second, the sample was strongly centred
on respondents reporting Spanish nationality and was dominated by
music-festival participation, which may limit transferability to other
national and event contexts. Third, although respondents reported
exposure to a variety of smart festival technologies, the technological
profile was largely dominated by functional and service-oriented
systems, meaning that the study captures aggregate
smart-technology-enabled festival experiences rather than the effects of
specific technological applications. Finally, given the cross-sectional
design and the less-than-ideal global fit of the structural model, the
findings should be interpreted with appropriate caution. Future research
could compare different categories of smart festival technologies,
examine variation across festival genres and national contexts, and use
longitudinal or event-based designs to better capture how
personalization, immersion, and loyalty evolve across the festival
experience.
